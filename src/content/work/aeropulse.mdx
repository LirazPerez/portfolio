## What I noticed

Nobody was short on equipment. They were short on proof.

Answering "is this a problem right now?" took three phone calls, because cables, batteries and shipments lived in separate spreadsheets that never agreed. So people did the safe thing: they approved the request and bought again. The duplicate purchase was never the real problem. It was what happens when nobody trusts the number.

## Problem

Most logistics tools treat equipment tracking as a counting problem. I treated it as a trust problem: the question is not how much there is, it is who will act on that number without checking it first.

- Dozens of active field sites across the US, coordinated with a supply-chain team in Israel.
- Two very different core users: a Logistics Manager who owns the whole picture, and an LDO embedded in one field team who needs to know a request is moving without chasing it.
- Every competitor I reviewed (Dronedesk, Airdata UAV, EZOfficeInventory) solves counting. None carries trust across a project boundary and an international border at the same time.

## How I ran it

- **I built the real system with the people who run it, not just for them.** The company's US logistics manager was my stakeholder and the system's owner. I designed the flows with him against how the operation really ran, and he runs it today.
- **I designed for everyone who touches equipment, not just the people who order it.** Each field team has one LDO who logs requests and counts. The warehouse team, the supply-chain team in Israel, regional managers, team leads, the head of operations and the VP all work from the same screens.
- **I started from the operation, not the screens.** The first artifact was a map of the real flow: field request, availability check, purchase or transfer, shipment, receipt.
- **I scoped to the three decisions that carry the thesis** and deliberately deferred anything that did not depend on them, including role-based navigation.

## Process

- Research: competitive and heuristic review, two personas, asset identification standards, a findings log.
- Information architecture: navigation model, screen inventory, key flows.
- Build in Base44, iterating on the request-to-fulfillment flow more than any other.
- **What didn't work: syncing demand to reality automatically.** I first had the numbers in a draft request flow straight into the live project inventory. They kept drifting apart, because what a team asks for is not what ends up on site.
- **What I did instead: a deliberate manual step.** I took the automation out and made moving numbers from a request into project inventory a manual action. A number the system calculates wrong on its own costs more than the minute it takes someone to enter the right one. It's the same principle as decision 3: a person stands behind the number before anyone acts on it.

## Decisions

### 1. Check availability before a request becomes a purchase

When an LDO files a shortage, the system checks every other active project before the request reaches procurement, and offers a transfer in the same form. The Logistics Manager doesn't have to trust that nothing was double-ordered. She can see it.

*Considered instead:* a low-stock alert that tells procurement to reorder. Rejected, because it solves counting and creates exactly the duplicate it was meant to prevent.

### 3. Confirm with evidence, not with a status

A shipment isn't marked arrived because its ETA passed. Someone attaches the delivery photo and the bill of lading and confirms it. HQ-sourced parts move forward through a manual Advance action.

*Trade-off:* more clicks for the person on site. Worth it, because a status nobody confirmed is the number nobody trusts.

## Build

**My part:** the operating model, the research, the information architecture, every decision above, and the iteration on the request flow.

**The tools' part:** Base44 generated the working app from my prompts, both the real system and this version. Claude helped turn my brief into research structure and build prompts.

**How it was decided:** direction and decisions from me, implementation from the tools, checked against the real operation with its logistics manager.

## Outcome

**Adopted across the whole operation.** 20 to 30 people, in every role above from LDOs in the field to the VP, work from it every day. It saves the team 8 to 10 hours of work a week, and planning and equipment tracking stopped depending on phone calls and parallel spreadsheets.

What's in the product: inventory across warehouse and sites, cable and battery stock with reorder thresholds, field requests with cross-project transfer, a shipment pipeline with evidence-based arrival, defect analysis, and US/Israel procurement tracking.

## Still open

- **Role-based navigation.** The sidebar doesn't yet separate the Logistics Manager's full view from the LDO's. For an LDO this is a side tool; for a Logistics Manager it's the whole job.
- **A freshness signal on availability.** A transfer approved against a stale "available" count could leave the other project short, exactly the failure the thesis exists to prevent. It needs a "last verified" marker, not just a number.
- **A usability test with a real logistics manager** on the request-to-fulfillment flow, the one I iterated on most without watching anyone else use it.
