What I noticed

Nobody was short on equipment. They were short on proof.

So people approved the request and bought again. The duplicate was never the problem; distrust was.

Process

I mapped the operation before drawing a single screen.

  1. 01Field requestAn LDO logs a shortage
  2. 02Availability checkEvery active project, first
  3. 03Transfer or purchaseTransfer offered in the same form
  4. 04ShipmentUS and Israel, tracked
  5. 05Receipt, with proofPhoto and bill of lading
✕
What didn't workDraft request numbers synced automatically into project inventory. They drifted: what a team asks for isn't what lands on site.
✓
What I did insteadA deliberate manual step. A wrong number the system made up costs more than a minute of someone entering the right one.
ההחלטות: מסך גדול לכל החלטה, כותרת ושורה אחת, ו"מה שקלתי במקום" בשורה קצרה.
Key decisions

Three decisions carry the whole idea.

1

Check availability before a request becomes a purchase.

The system looks across every active project and offers a transfer in the same form.

Considered: a low-stock alert to procurement.Rejected, because it creates exactly the duplicate it tries to prevent.
2

Match the alert to how urgent it actually is.

Recoverable stays in the row. Urgent and unexplained goes straight to the evidence.

Considered: one alert style for everything.Rejected, because when everything is red, nothing is.
3

Confirm with evidence, not with a status.

A shipment is marked arrived when someone attaches the photo and the bill of lading.

Trade-off: more clicks on site.Worth it, because a status nobody confirmed is the number nobody trusts.
